The Consultant's Proposal & EOI Response Kit

Win more consulting work: qualify the right bids, answer expressions of interest that get shortlisted, and write proposals clients say yes to.

By Coach David Adeoye Abodunrin
AI & Automation Coach · Master Futurist · Founder, DeoCoach
The promise. Most consultants lose bids before they write a word: they chase the wrong ones, ignore the evaluation criteria, or send a CV where the buyer wanted evidence. This kit gives you a bid/no-bid filter, an EOI structure, a proposal template and AI prompts that do the heavy lifting.

Step 1 — Bid or no bid? (score in 10 minutes)

Score each line 0, 1 or 2. Bid if you score 12 or more. Between 8 and 11, bid only with a partner who fills your gaps. Under 8, walk away and spend the time on a better opportunity.

Step 2 — Answer the Expression of Interest

An EOI decides who is shortlisted. Evaluators score against published criteria, usually firm experience, relevant assignments and key staff. Mirror their headings exactly so they can find the points.

  1. Cover letter (one page). Name the assignment and reference number, state your interest, and give three reasons you are the right firm in one sentence each.
  2. Firm profile (one page). Legal name, registration, years in operation, offices, headcount, and the services that match this assignment. Cut everything else.
  3. Relevant assignments (the section that wins). For each one: client, country, dates, value, your role, what you delivered and the measurable result. Put the most similar assignments first.
  4. Key experts. Short CVs in the buyer's format, with each person's experience matched to the role they will play.
  5. Partners (if any). Who they are, what gap they fill, and a signed letter of association.
The shortlist rule. Evaluators reward evidence that is similar in sector, size and geography. "We have done exactly this, here, at this scale" beats a long list of general experience.

Step 3 — Write the proposal

SectionWhat it must do
1. UnderstandingRestate the client's problem better than they did, including what happens if it is not solved. Show you read the terms of reference closely.
2. Comments on the terms of referenceRespectfully point out gaps, risks or better options. This is where you show judgement.
3. Approach and methodologyPhases, activities and outputs, each tied to an objective in their terms of reference.
4. Work planA timeline by week or month, with milestones and deliverables.
5. TeamWho does what, how many days each, and why they are the right person.
6. Risks and qualityThe five biggest risks, how you will manage them, and how you will assure quality.
7. FeesClear, complete and in their format: day rates, days, expenses, taxes. No surprises.

Step 4 — Price with confidence

  • Build up, then check down. Days × day rate + expenses + contingency, then compare with what the buyer can likely pay.
  • Offer options where the rules allow: a core scope, and an enhanced scope with more impact.
  • Price the outcome, not the hours on private-sector work: what is the result worth to the client?
